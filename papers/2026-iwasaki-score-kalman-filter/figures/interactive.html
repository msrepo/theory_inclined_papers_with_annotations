1. Score matching is one linear solve

Pick a target density and a polynomial degree r. The page computes the target's moments mk = E[xk], builds A (entries jk·mj+k−2) and b (entries j(j−1)·mj−2), and solves Aλ = b. That is Proposition 1: no normaliser, no iterations. For comparison it also runs MaxEnt, which matches the moments exactly but needs the normaliser Z(λ) at every Newton step. That is cheap in 1-D and costs Gn in n dimensions.

MaxEnt keeps improving. The right panel shows why: the fit only has to follow −log p where the data are.

2. Stein's identity: moments from λ, and a closure that breaks

2a. The identity is a recursion

For p ∝ e−E with a polynomial energy, E[ −E′(x)·xk + k·xk−1 ] = 0 for every k. With E′ of degree r−1, each value of k links r+1 consecutive moments, so the lower ones generate all the higher ones. For a Gaussian it is mk+1 = μ·mk + kσ²·mk−1.

The nudged seed still satisfies every Stein equation, yet it produces a moment sequence that no density in the family has. So the identities alone do not pin down the moments: you also need the right seeds. That is exactly the difficulty in recovering moments after an update (section 3).

2b. The closure through a change of modality

A 1-D double well, dx = (x − x³)dt + σdW, started as a narrow bump on the hilltop. The density splits into two lumps at ±1. The moment ODE ṁk = k·mk − k·mk+2 + ½σ²k(k−1)·mk−2 needs two degrees beyond the budget K = 2r−2 (excess degree 2). So each step score-matches λ from m0..K and gets mK+1, mK+2 from two Stein layers. Monte Carlo with 20,000 particles is the reference. This is a 1-D analogue of Appendix L, in raw coordinates.

Try it. At r = 2, Gaussian closure runs to the end but settles on the wrong variance, because one bump cannot sit in two wells. At r = 4 the fitted density does split, but the moments drift away from Monte Carlo as the split happens. At r = 6 the closed ODE diverges almost at once. The start is Gaussian, so the fitted leading coefficient λ6 begins at zero and stays small, and every Stein layer divides by it (the readout shows its value). App L's mechanism is the same division: the closure is fragile wherever the leading coefficient is small, near a Gaussian or near a change of modality. Higher r fails sooner, as the paper reports.

3. The measurement update, step by step

Bayes' rule in score form is addition: λ⁺ = λ⁻ + λlik. That step is exact whenever the log-likelihood is a polynomial of degree ≤ r, and the blue curve below is its result, computed by quadrature. The filter then needs the posterior's moments for the next prediction. Here you choose how they are recovered, then watch what the refinement loop λ ← SM(m⁺) + λlik does.

Start with the defaults: Gaussian prior N(0, 1), linear sensor, z = 2, R = 1, r = 2. The exact posterior is N(1, 0.5). Stein as §5.2 recovers a mean of −0.25: truncation sets E[x³] = 0 about the prior mean, but for the posterior it is 2.5. Switch to + β = 0 row or oracle and the mean comes back. Then pick exact and press + refinement pass a few times. The posterior narrows and slides towards z, because each pass adds λlik again. With §5.2's recovery, passes pull the mean back to the prior instead. For a quadratic sensor (needs r ≥ 4), try z = 2. The posterior has two modes near ±1.2, and λ⁺ = λ⁻ + λlik represents them exactly. Recovering its moments is another matter: every Stein variant returns a negative variance. By symmetry the mean is right, so centring cannot help. Truncation sets m8 ≈ 14 to zero in the row where it carries the leading coefficient.

4. The oscillator benchmark: who wins depends on the truth

Two coupled "Duffing" oscillators (n = 4), with the paper's parameters: γ = 0.3, α = 1, β = 0.6, κ = 0.3, process noise σ = 0.4 on the momenta. The prior is N((0.3, −0.2, 0, 0), 0.15²I). q₁ is measured every 0.15 s with R = 0.3², for 25 steps. The filter is an EKF with the correct model, except that its measurement noise is divided by a trust factor: trust 1 is correctly tuned, trust 0.01 barely listens to the sensor.

With the noise-free truth, slide trust down. The blue EKF stops chasing the noisy dots, hugs the green curve, and its error falls below the paper's SKF value around trust 0.03. Nothing about the filter improved: the truth simply is the prior's noise-free forecast. Now switch to drawn from the model. The truth wanders, low trust loses track, and the best trust is 1. Every error is above 0.2. That level is set by the process noise, and no filter can beat the posterior mean, which the PF approximates. (This page uses its own random numbers, so values differ a little from oscillator_benchmark.py, which gives 0.0657 at trust 1 and 0.0103 at trust 0.03 for the noise-free truth, and 0.225 at trust 1 over 40 seeds for the model-drawn one.)
